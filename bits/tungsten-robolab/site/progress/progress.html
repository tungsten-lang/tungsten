Sprint

In progress v4: three bodies in

Body. Average, 95th percentile and literature elite bodies: the calibrated v4 body for the newest runners, the v3 anatomy for the older ones.

Full drive on the prime movers, with the opposing muscles scaled down as their partners fire (reciprocal inhibition, k 0.5), latched on each foot. On the calibrated v4 body the 95th-percentile runner reaches 7.91 m/s (12 of 12) with four gears, the literature elite body 6.79 with three and the average body 6.17 with three. On the older v3 body the same design reaches 7.02, 6.56 and 5.05. The inhibition is worth +0.12 m/s on every start (v3, 95th percentile), and without it the gait falls in 4.4 to 5.6 seconds.

An earlier ceiling of about 10.2 m/s for the 95th-percentile body rested on an ideal torso stabiliser that applies up to 3.9 kN·m in a 0.12 s stance, where the stance hip's muscles give at most 185 N·m. Ceilings are now computed with the torso free. On the original body that put the average runner's ceiling at 6.89 m/s, below the 7.8 of recreationally active adults, so the body model was calibrated against published data (the v4 body, below). On v4 the ceilings are 8.2, 10.45 and 9.28 m/s.

The v4 95th-percentile runner is at 76% of its ceiling, the average-body runner at 75% and the elite runner at 73%. The average-body gait was found by starting the search from the 95th-percentile gait (it replaced a straight-leg 4.65 m/s gait). One parameter set cannot both accelerate a standing body and cruise: a gait tuned from flying starts at top speed ran 0.5 m/s faster and falls from a standing start. So every tunable has a twin per gear, and above a speed (6.5, 7.0 and 7.7 m/s for the 95th-percentile runner) the whole stride is rebuilt from the next gear's values at the next flight; each gear is searched from flying starts recorded on the gear before it. The gains per gear were +0.45, +0.41 and +0.05 m/s. Falls on 40 unseen starts, two seed sets: average 0 and 5 (four of them at 5-7 s), 95th percentile 2 and 1, elite 1 and 2.

Why not 10 m/s: at its best the 95th-percentile runner is in contact for 0.102 s, flies for 0.139 s and takes about 4.15 steps a second of about 1.9 m, with mean and peak ground force of 2.35 and 4.5 body weights. The ideal at the 10.45 ceiling is in contact for 0.100 s, flies for 0.161 s and takes 3.83 steps a second of 2.61 m, at 2.61 and 5.06 body weights. Force and contact time are within 10% of the ideal, but the runner flies 14% less and steps 27% shorter. The search never adopted the ideal stance because the extended stance falls in acceleration on 60% of standing starts: no controller here holds the trunk against the stance hip's extension torque (it pitches 0.36 rad per stance, against the ideal's 0.1 rad).

Jumping and hurdles

Long jump

In progress Oracle-class programs

Body. v3 anatomy, 95th percentile (elite variants: the elite and elite2 profiles); the calibrated v4 anatomy for the new v4 jump.

The run-up is the sprint champion itself. World Athletics rules are in the score: foul line on every tick of the stance, no somersault past 120° until landing is over, no touching the runway after landing, and the mark is the nearest break in the sand by any body part. The take-off comes from an ideal-control analysis of the runner's own approach, distilled into contraction knots latched on the plant touchdown.

That took the 95th-percentile body from 3.68 m to 4.895 m on one start (99% of that start's 4.93 m ideal take-off; the 7.02 m/s runner). A landing program learned for that one start then took it to 5.122 m, with the mark 0.37 m beyond the centre of mass's ballistic 4.75 m. That landing is an open-loop excitation table on a clock locked to the predicted landing, so it is oracle-class and overfit to the start by construction.

The robust champion, over 16 perturbed starts, went from 3.22 m to 3.92 m and now to 4.63 m: an ideal-control take-off launched from the penultimate touchdown, a plant latch at 1.5 body weights, a swing-foot lag feedback, and a landing program learned on 32 hand-overs, on a ballistic-time clock (the landing program's clock used to start at the hand-over, so a slightly lower or slower flight landed 30 to 50 ms early with the legs still hanging and the body vaulted over its feet). Both programs are near open loop (reflex gain 0.03), so this one is oracle-class too. On starts no search ever used it scores 4.55 m over 32 (31 legal) and 4.32 m over 96 (89 legal); a separate check of 384 fresh starts gave 95.8% legal, 2.3% somersaults and a 4.48 m mean, and the 28 Sep champion scored 3.27 m on the first 96. The literature elite body reaches 3.59 m over 32 fresh starts (31 legal), up from 3.01 m.

The first jump on the calibrated v4 body (7.0 m/s runner, ideal take-off 4.89 m) was 4.945 m on one start. With the two-gear runner (6.58 m/s over the last 5 m at a 44 m check mark, was 6.40) and an ideal take-off learned for that start (5.219 m ballistic from the foot, open-loop excitations of all 32 muscles, oracle-class), the one-start jump is 5.209 m. The final robust programs (hip-nudge run-up steering, an upright final approach, a take-off program trained on both plant sides over gap-stratified approaches, a landing table trained on 117 hand-overs and a 0.03 m check-mark shift; all near open loop, oracle-class) score 3.41 m over 16 starts (12 legal), 4.50 m over 32 fresh starts (31 legal) and 3.72 m over 96 (77 legal; the earlier programs: 2.15, 2.85 and 2.54). On 480 starts that no choice ever used the mean is 4.00 m (415 legal, 86.5%; legal median 4.70 m, 10th to 90th percentile 4.38 to 4.83 m, best 4.92 m); the check-mark shift alone is worth +0.35 m there (fouls 48 to 3). The average body reaches 1.85 m robust and 2.47 m on one start.

The jump is not beyond human. The best mark, 5.209 m on one start, is 105% of the untrained man's 4.95 m and overfit to that start; the robust mean is about 4.0 m, 81% of it. What limits the robust jump: stumbles in the steered approach (about 11% of fresh starts, about 0.5 m of the mean); one take-off program for all starts, which reaches a vertical take-off speed of 2.1 to 2.4 m/s where the ideal has 3.1; somersaults after weak short flights (2 to 6%); and speed at the board (6.6 m/s over the last 5 m; the runner only reaches 6.6 to 6.9 m/s at 44 m, so its third and fourth gears never engage). Ideal-control what-ifs on one start: +0.4 / +0.8 m/s of speed gives 5.22 / 5.52 m, 20% / 40% stronger muscles give 5.43 / 5.91 m (+0.39 / +0.88 m: strength is spent on lift), a 65 m run-up adds about 0.2 m (extrapolated). An acceleration gear for 3 to 7 m/s would move the jump; a fourth gear would not. The most promising untried lever is a run-up steering horizon of 6 strides, which cuts falls in the run from 26 to 9 of 288 starts but needs a retrained landing table.

Ball skills

Baseball swing

Champion Above the open-loop ceiling

Body. Athlete body: strength ×1.5, every muscle's peak speed 3.0 L0/s, a pivot root with a yaw hinge and a slide, no whole-body balance. On the human-calibrated body at strength 1.0 the champion is 10.3 m/s.

The earlier hitch-free swing was slow because of its plan. The arms and wrists fired at full power only 2 to 8% of the swing, the bottom wrist cast early, and the bat peaked 42 ms before contact. The fast swing turns the pelvis at full drive into its yaw limit, keeps the spine coiled, fires short arm bursts with no co-contraction, and whips the bottom wrist through 0.8 rad in the last 60 ms. That wrist runs at 1,069 °/s in the open-loop program and 930 °/s in the reflex-on swing; college hitters manage 1,093 ± 354 (lower hand).

The open-loop program scores 25.2 m/s on 24 fresh pitches and hits all 24. It was built from 6 of the 18 grid pitches (their locations at 76 mph), so the 24 fresh pitches are the held-out test. Distilled into contraction knots with the reflexes on, it kept 82 to 85% of that speed (21.6 m/s, 2 to 4 of 24 pitches missed by 1 to 3 cm), and on a newer set of 24 held-out pitches it scored 19.4 m/s and hit 20 (the four misses were all low pitches, 0.64 to 0.67 m up). Landing the step 62 ms later had cut the pause before the swing from 115 ms to 50 ms.

Retuned by sep-CMA on 16 maximin fresh training pitches that no arena trial uses, the swing in robolab's own representation went from 19.4 to 25.7 and then, over five more searches (taim aim correction, arm reflex damping 0, an earlier and longer swing with the plan carried on past the program's contact, a drive time profile, nine leg and stride parameters), to 26.50 m/s on the 24 held-out pitches, all hit. The aim correction alone turned 36 of 48 hits into 48 of 48 (the misses were low pitches with aim errors of 7 to 18 cm, linear in the blend's location mismatch), and the drive profile added 0.42 m/s on all 48 untouched pitches. On 48 pitches nobody used until the end the champion hits 48 of 48 at 26.83 m/s, against 17.70 (36 of 48) for the v6 knots. It also holds the old open-loop trials in representation: 26.70 on the 18 grid pitches and 26.86 on the 24 earlier fresh pitches (the open-loop program: 25.17 and 25.24). The hitch metrics improved: mean flow 2.79 to 2.30, the stall before the swing 31 to 8 ms, plant to hips firing 51 to 17 ms. It is not an open-loop program: the contraction knots and the reflex layer are in the loop, and an open-loop search seeded from the swing's own contact point reaches only 22.7 where the swing itself gets 27.2.

What limits it: the pelvis turns at 437°/s (MLB 714), the torso at 567°/s (937), the bottom elbow at 1,042°/s (professionals 1,539), the bottom wrist at 1,065°/s (college 1,093 ± 354). None is near its speed limit or its range end, except the wrists; the prime movers are at 0.9 activation for only 14 to 51% of the 220 ms power phase. With the controller fixed and the aim re-centred, a stronger body gains about 2 m/s per +20% strength or muscle speed (strength ×1.2 / ×1.33 / ×1.5: 28.7 / 29.6 / 30.4; arms ×1.5: 28.4; trunk ×1.5: 27.7; elbows ×2: 27.6). The literature 95th-percentile body (a labelled variant with its own trial) reaches 32.4 m/s on untouched pitches, the MLB average, at the price of ball quality: about a fifth of its hits leave above 60°. One caveat on the athlete champion: it is 0.1 to 0.2 m/s faster than the swing before it (params/bat_r2_s3.txt) but makes fewer fair balls (143 of 162 over five sets against 153), so the earlier swing is the better choice when ball quality matters.

Table tennis

Blocked

Body. The official MyoSuite table-tennis body: 273 muscles and 2 pelvis actuators.

Predicts the ball through the bounce, slides the pelvis onto its path, latches the ready posture on time-to-paddle and sets the paddle face from an aim law. Phase 1 is 100%. Phase 2 is 40% over 300 serves (33% this morning), and the winner scored 64%. Round 10 re-tuned the backhand with a bounce-tick guard, a 17-factor screening design and response-surface runs: on 900 fresh serves the champion's 30.2% became 37.7% (backhand 21.8% to 35.9%, p below 0.005 on both blocks), and the arena's 300 serves went from 33.0% to 40.0% (p 0.002). A later variant (c5) does better still on fresh serves (900: 40.9%) but the arena cannot tell it from the champion (121 against 120 of 300, p 0.5): the arena's 300 seeds flatter the old champion.

The gain came from one defect. On the single tick when the table-contact flag is set the ball's velocity is mid-reversal, and the skill predicted the pad-plane crossing from it as if it were the bounce's outcome, so for that tick the plan was 0.3 to 1.6 m off on every serve (fixing it alone lifts the hit rate from 0.80 to 0.87), and it had hidden a small retreat that the champion depended on, which a late pelvis kick now restores on purpose. The ceiling is set by the arm: pad pitch 10° (1 sigma), pad velocity 0.5 m/s and a 5 cm miss of the sweet spot cap even a perfect plan near 45%, against 90% needing about 4°, 0.25 m/s and 3 cm at once. Fast serves are hit but come back too fast: above 5.5 m/s only 8 to 13° of pad pitch return a ball that clears the net and lands.

The aim is accurate. The arm does not carry it out: a forehand asked to turn its forearm 1.20 rad reaches 0.44, and the face follows 0.53 to 0.66 of an aimed change on the forehand and 0.13 to 0.28 on the backhand (1 would be perfect). Live moment arms fix the arm's tracking (backhand error 0.80 to 0.21 rad, forehand yaw follow 0.66 to 1.01), but the tracked arm plays like a new player and needs a long practice run. Where the ball meets the paddle decides most forehands: 67% solved within 3 cm of the centre, 1 in 7 on the rim.

Your strokes: the chop goes high because the aim rule opens the forehand face about 40° and the forehand path cannot get steeper than about 40° down. The best chop is the backhand, commanded at a 60° face and a 40° downward path, which reaches −4° and 33° down (4 cm over the net, 11 rev/s of backspin, 3 of 11 landed). The wrist flick makes topspin, under 5 rev/s, because its paddle reaches only 1.3 to 1.5 m/s.

The hold is steered by the sensed centre of pressure, with a slow centring loop and per-posture muscle moment arms. The centring loop is what carries a hold past 96 s. On the 40-start strict trial (310 s episodes), 34 of 40 fresh stances stay on one leg for at least 300 s, against 24 of 40 for the previous champion. The new champion has the lift into single support tuned (sep-CMA on an 8 s proxy, 16 fresh starts per generation). On the older 20-start trial the previous champion holds 12 of 20, and the same lift scored 15 of 20 there (p 0.23). The short two-leg trial (3 s on each leg, at most 6) went from 1.96 to 3.08 s, with the right leg still the weak one (0.63 s). The routine also holds the right leg first: 8 of 8 starts hold 310 s.

Round 4 overturned the first diagnosis: in single support the standing foot's heading stays within 0.02 rad, so it does not spin. What was wrong was the controller's hip model, measured at the start posture: the gluteal muscles' moment arms change sign where one-leg stance happens (one muscle's rotation torque per unit activation runs from −29 to +37 N·m). On the 20 starts every failure was at the lift: the swing foot leaves the ground still carrying 20 to 40% of body weight, which throws the capture point 5 to 6 cm outward, past the ±2 cm the sole can absorb. On 40 new starts, 5 fell within 20 s: one in the lift, one in the set, three in the hold after the lift, where the pelvis yaw sits at the stance hip's stop, goes into a 0.75 s limit cycle and the frontal plane then runs away.

Round 8 found the cause of most early falls: an over-shift. At the first lift tick the stance foot's lateral capture point separates the outcomes: 0 of 212 lifts fell with it at or inboard of the foot centre, 11 of 28 fell with it outboard (240 starts). The feed-forward shift moves the body's centre the same amount whatever the stance width, so a wide stance overshoots. Waiting for the capture point to return is fatal (0 fixed, 13 broke: they fall in the shift). A capture-point brake inside the shift fixed 11 of 11 failing outboard starts, and on unseen starts took 112 to 119 of 120 holding 20 s (p 0.02). The 310 s trial did not reward it (35 of 40) because of blow-ups: MuJoCo reports 'Nan, Inf or huge value in QACC' inside a motionless hold, the posture constant to 3 decimals for 130 s, in 7 of 120 arena runs. Every hold carries a steady 215 N shank-against-thigh contact (1.1 mm penetration, 40 of 40 starts) and a swing-ankle limit cycle; neither was shown to cause it, and candidates with less contact lose hold robustness. A challenger now needs 5 fixes and 0 breaks to be promoted (p 0.031), and one blow-up among the 34 kept starts loses it.

Round 9 found the cause of the blow-ups. At the failing step exactly one muscle length is NaN: the swing leg's right rectus femoris, whose line wraps a cylinder at the knee. MuJoCo's wrap code takes the arccosine of the dot product of the two tangent points; when the muscle line only grazes the cylinder (within about a nanometre of the tangent) the points are 2e-8 rad apart, the dot rounds above 1.0 about one time in ten, and the NaN goes on to the muscle force and the whole acceleration. The swing-leg muscle hovers at that tangent and crosses it hundreds of times, so a hold is a lottery of about 0.1 per visit: it looked rare and chaotic. The contact, the solver and the time step were excluded (the NaN survives every one of them; only wrap-geometry changes remove it), and the same reproduction gives the same NaNs on MuJoCo 3.2.7, 3.3.0, 3.6.0 and 3.14.0. A guard that redoes the substep after a nudge of at most 1e-10 rad is bit-identical to the official environment until a NaN, and every one of the 10 events it met was survived. Guarded, the ranking flips (lift 35, ramp 39, shift brake 40 of 40 in the five-minute trial): the brake had lost five holds to the bug, while the lift's five falls are real balance falls. On 120 fresh starts at 20 s the ramp beats the lift (113 against 102 of 120, p 0.006) and the brake holds 118. The ramp family parks the swing leg's rectus femoris at the tangent about three times as often as the lift, which explains its unlucky official counts.

A second victim turned up in the guarded runs: a trunk muscle on another wrap cylinder went NaN at 2.1 s in start 96029, which the official environment would score as a balance failure.

Search methods · finished

Genetic, random walk, or something else?

Thirteen optimizers behind one interface, equal rollout budgets, several seeds and held-out re-checks, on a jump, push-recovery balance on the 80-muscle leg, and refining the run.

QuestionAnswer
Genetic algorithm?No. GA, differential evolution, cross-entropy, MAP-Elites, Nelder-Mead and random search all failed to improve on the jump.
Random walk?Yes for ballistic moves: the (1+1) random walk had the best median jump and the most robust run refinement, but it overfits badly when trained on fresh random perturbations.
Best for choreographed motionBayesian optimisation or the random walk over goal posture + duration: 0.71 m median jump vs 0.13 m with rate vectors.
Best for robust balanceSeparable CMA-ES, trained on 4+ perturbations (falls on unseen pushes 12/30 to 3/30).
Contraction vectorsNever search raw per-muscle values; synergies distilled from good solutions converge fastest.
Keyframes, your ideaIt won. Converting the best posture solution into contraction keyframes and refining them in muscle space took the jump from 0.91 m to 1.3 to 1.5 m.
Since thenQuintic knots with velocity and acceleration vectors, a small learned correction from proprioception (legal jump 1.27 to 1.33 m with no fall on any arena start), and a gait stride as a knot train fitted by least squares then improved by learning control.
Under the hood

Bodies and arena

  • v2 anatomy: toe joints, biarticular muscles on fixed tendons, human peak joint speeds (elbow 35, knee 25, hip 15 rad/s), human-scale joint damping. Opt-in, so older results reproduce exactly.
  • v3 adds elastic Achilles and quadriceps tendons; population bodies (average, 95th percentile, elite) and the calibrated v4 body are listed above.
  • The arena holds 97 trials on fixed starts. A challenger replaces the champion on a paired sign-flip test (p < 0.05), or on any strict gain in a one-start trial, and every result keeps the note of the approach that produced it.

SDK and compiler

  • Chunk spring times, joint corrections at limits and a dead tuning parameter were fixed in the SDK; branch, followed_by, stagger and via keyframes were added.
  • Internal-model gravity feed-forward, a clamped reflex integral (postural tone), exact posture-to-contraction geometry for coupled joints and optional articulated fingers.
  • Knot trains (RunKnotsSkill): a stride as quintic knots at its gait events, fitted with zero rollouts to 0.1 to 0.6° and 20 to 90 times better than min-jerk keyframes at the same events.
  • Two compiler fixes are merged into main: methods with more than 8 parameters, and parameters named t0 or t1 colliding with LLVM temporaries (which also fixed a use-after-free).
